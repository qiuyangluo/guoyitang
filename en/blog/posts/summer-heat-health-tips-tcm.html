Summer health tips: prevent heat stress, protect digestion, and sleep steadily

New York summers can combine humidity, air conditioning, iced drinks, and late nights. A steady rhythm for fluids, meals, movement, and rest helps the body handle the season more comfortably.

June 18, 2026 · Guoyitang Health Column

Short answer: Summer care is not about chasing cold at every moment. From a TCM perspective, the practical priorities are preventing heat stress, protecting digestion, and calming the spirit. Hydrate regularly, limit excessive iced drinks and greasy late meals, avoid direct cold air, change after heavy sweating, and slow down before bed. Seek urgent care for high fever, confusion, repeated vomiting, chest tightness, shortness of breath, or clear dehydration.

This article is for people who feel tired, low in appetite, restless at night, sensitive to heat, or easily chilled by air conditioning in summer. In TCM, "nourishing yang in spring and summer" does not mean heavy tonics. It means respecting the season's outward movement, natural sweating, and warmer weather while avoiding habits that overburden digestion. This is general education only and does not replace an exam, diagnosis, or prescription.

1. Hydrate often, but do not depend on large iced drinks

Summer sweating removes both fluid and electrolytes. Many people wait until they are thirsty, then drink a large amount of ice water at once. It feels cooling for a moment, but for some people it can irritate the stomach and contribute to bloating, loose stools, or reduced appetite. A gentler plan is to drink smaller amounts throughout the day, especially before going outside, exercising, cooking, or caring for older adults and children.

  • Choose room-temperature water, light tea, or small amounts of warm fluids for daily use.
  • After heavy sweating, consider electrolyte replacement; high-sugar drinks should not become your main water source.
  • If you have heart or kidney disease, fluid restrictions, or take diuretics, follow your clinician's hydration guidance.

2. Keep meals light, cooked, and digestion-friendly

Summer eating does not have to mean only salads, fruit, and cold foods. In TCM, the Spleen and Stomach prefer warmth and regularity. In humid heat, frequent smoothies, raw seafood, late-night barbecue, and sweet milk tea may worsen a "damp-heavy" feeling: body heaviness, sticky mouth, post-meal bloating, or sticky stools. Simple home meals often work better: rice or congee, cooked vegetables, beans, and moderate fish or meat, stopping before you feel overfull.

Note: Mung bean soup, coix seed, adzuki bean, winter melon, and similar foods are often used in summer food therapy, but they are not suitable for everyone in large amounts. Pregnant patients, people who feel cold easily, people with frequent diarrhea, and anyone taking medications should ask a clinician first.

3. Use air conditioning, but avoid direct cold wind

Air conditioning can prevent heat illness and help older adults or people with chronic conditions get through hot weather safely. Problems often come from a large indoor-outdoor temperature gap, direct cold air, or entering a cold room immediately after heavy sweating. Chilling the neck, shoulders, low back, abdomen, knees, or ankles may leave some people feeling tight, achy, or uncomfortable.

  • Keep vents from blowing directly onto beds, sofas, or workstations.
  • After sweating heavily, dry off and change into dry clothes before sitting in a cold room.
  • At night, keep the temperature moderate and cover the abdomen and joints with a light blanket.

4. Exercise for a light sweat, not exhaustion

Moderate sweating can help the body release heat and keep qi moving, but midday heat is not the time for intense outdoor training. Early morning or evening walks, Ba Duan Jin, tai chi, and light strength work are often more sustainable. After exercise, let breathing, heart rate, and sweating settle before taking a cold shower or drinking a large iced beverage.

5. Protect sleep by slowing down before bed

Longer days can lead to later nights, restlessness, and lighter sleep. TCM often connects summer heat with an unsettled heart-spirit. Reducing phone stimulation one hour before bed, keeping dinner moderate, and writing tomorrow's tasks down can be more effective than trying to force yourself to sleep on command. If insomnia lasts more than three weeks, or comes with anxiety, palpitations, or significant fatigue, consider medical evaluation.

6. When is wellness care not enough?

Seasonal care is for prevention and mild discomfort. Seek modern medical evaluation for persistent high fever, confusion, chest pain, shortness of breath, repeated vomiting, inability to drink fluids, very low urine output, bloody or black stools, severe abdominal pain, or a major change in alertness in an older adult or child. Heat illness, infection, and cardiovascular problems can all be mistaken for "summer heat" or "dampness."

Conclusion

The center of summer care is balance: heat, humidity, sweat, and cold air all ask something different from the body. Start with sustainable steps: regular fluids, cooked light meals, fewer iced drinks and late snacks, air conditioning without direct wind, and a calmer bedtime. If symptoms repeat, Guoyitang in Flushing can discuss whether acupuncture, herbs, or lifestyle care may fit your pattern after an in-person evaluation.

Disclaimer: This article is for health education and does not constitute medical advice. Diagnosis and treatment plans vary from person to person. If you feel unwell, please seek medical treatment in time or have an interview with a licensed traditional Chinese medicine practitioner.
